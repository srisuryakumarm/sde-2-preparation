## Theory Block (45 min)

### The core mechanics
- **Anchoring:** whoever states a number first sets the frame the rest of the conversation orbits around. If a recruiter asks for a number before an offer exists, the standard move is to redirect — "I'd rather hear the full package first so we're comparing apples to apples" — rather than naming a figure that then becomes a ceiling.
- **BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement):** the honest answer to "what happens if this specific negotiation fails" is what actually determines how hard a position can be pushed. A live second offer is a strong BATNA. A vague sense that "the market is fine" is a weak one. This is precisely why Day 118's strategy — applying to all 7 targets in the same window — matters: it's what turns a weak BATNA into a strong one by the time any single offer actually arrives.
- **Using a competing offer without bluffing:** the honest version of this move is stating a real number from a real, verifiable offer, and asking the other company to match or beat it — not inventing a number that doesn't exist. Bluffing is discoverable (recruiters compare notes more than candidates expect) and the downside (a rescinded offer, a burned relationship) is asymmetric to the upside.
- **Never negotiate against yourself.** If a number is countered, the next move belongs to the other side. Filling the silence with a second concession before they've responded to the first one gives away the whole point of countering.
- **Everything is negotiable, not just base:** in Indian tech comp specifically, base/fixed CTC, joining bonus, variable pay structure, ESOP/RSU grant size and vesting cliff, notice-period buyout (a real, common negotiation point when moving between Indian tech employers), and start date are all separately movable. A company that says "we can't move base" may still move joining bonus or buyout coverage — worth asking about each lever separately rather than accepting a single blanket "no."
- **Public vs. newer-private company RSU considerations:** Google and Walmart Global Tech issue standard, liquid public-company RSUs — straightforward to value. Rippling, Databricks, and Stripe are privately held (Databricks and Stripe have both discussed public-market activity at various points, so check current status before any offer conversation) — equity there requires asking direct questions: current 409A valuation, vesting schedule, and realistically how and when it becomes liquid. Treat an equity number at a private company as informational, not as cash-equivalent, until there's a clear answer on liquidity.

### Scripts

**Buying time on an initial offer call** (never negotiate on the spot, even if the number sounds good):
> "Thank you, this is genuinely exciting. I want to give it the attention it deserves rather than reacting on the spot — can I take 2–3 days and come back to you?"

**Countering, once ready:**
> "I've had a chance to think this through and I'm excited about the role. Based on [the scope of the role / a competing offer at $X / market data for this level], I was hoping we could get closer to $Y on the base, with the rest of the package as proposed. Is there flexibility there?"

**Using a real competing offer (never invent one):**
> "I want to be transparent — I have another offer at $X. [Company] is genuinely my first choice, and I'd love to find a way to make this work without needing to go back and forth. Is there room to close that gap?"

**Handling "this is our best and final":**
> "I appreciate you being direct about that. Before I give a final answer, is there any flexibility on [joining bonus / equity refresh timing / notice-period buyout] even if base is fixed?" — a "best and final" on one lever doesn't mean every lever is closed.

**Handling a counter-offer from the current employer** (this is a real decision point, not just a negotiation tactic — most people who accept a counter-offer to stay end up leaving within 6–12 months anyway, because the original reasons for leaving rarely get fixed by a raise alone):
> Worth deciding in advance, calmly, *why* the search started in the first place — comp, growth ceiling, tech stack, company trajectory — and being honest about whether a counter-offer actually fixes that reason or just delays the same conversation.

### Common mistakes to avoid
- Revealing the lower bound of an acceptable range first.
- Treating the recruiter as an adversary rather than a collaborator who usually also wants the deal to close — the more useful frame is "help me get this over the line internally," not "beat them."
- Accepting or rejecting on the first call, before running the numbers or sleeping on it.
- Negotiating over email when a call would land better for anything with real back-and-forth — email is fine for confirming numbers after a verbal agreement, weaker for the actual back-and-forth itself.
- Letting an "exploding offer" (a deadline of 24–48 hours) force a rushed decision — a reasonable ask for a few more days is almost always granted; if it genuinely isn't, that itself is useful information about the company.

## Practice Structure (45–60 min)

**Role-play with the accountability partner**, three short rounds, partner playing the recruiter each time with a different secret posture (don't reveal which in advance):

1. **Round 1 — Partner has real flexibility** (a genuine budget range, motivated to close). Practice countering cleanly, using the scripts above as scaffolding rather than reading them verbatim.
2. **Round 2 — Partner plays "best and final" on base, but hasn't been asked about other levers.** Practice pivoting to joining bonus / equity / buyout instead of repeating the same ask.
3. **Round 3 — Partner introduces a 24-hour exploding deadline mid-negotiation.** Practice asking for more time without sounding like a red flag.

After each round, 2–3 minutes of partner feedback: did it sound rehearsed and stiff, or natural? Was the ask specific (a number, a lever) or vague ("more money")? Did the tone stay collaborative under pressure?
