# Gap Fill: Day 140A — The "Tell Me About Yourself" Pitch

**Gap addressed (#3):** every single loop, at every company, opens with some version of this question. Week 21 gives 8 dedicated rehearsal slots to individual STAR stories, but nothing anywhere builds the 60–90 second career narrative that comes *before* all of them — the thing that sets the frame the interviewer carries into everything that follows.

**How this slots in:** one standalone session, inserted between Day 140 (Capstone close, Sunday) and Day 141 (Week 21 Day 1 — STAR framework + competency map). Placed deliberately *before* the STAR story work begins, not during or after it — the idea is to build the overall throughline first, so the 8 STAR stories built across the rest of the week can each be framed as an illustration of that throughline rather than eight disconnected anecdotes.

---

## Why this matters more than it seems

A rambling or unfocused answer here doesn't just waste the first two minutes — it actively shapes which follow-up questions get asked, which parts of the resume get probed, and how generously ambiguous answers later in the loop get interpreted. A tight, deliberate pitch is one of the highest-leverage 90 seconds in the entire interview process, precisely because it's the one moment where the *candidate*, not the interviewer, controls what gets emphasized.

## The structure: Present → Past → Future

A reliable shape that avoids both extremes (starting at birth, or a single flat sentence):

1. **Present (15–20 sec):** who you are right now, professionally, in one clean sentence. Role, core stack, what you're known for on a team.
2. **Past (30–40 sec):** the arc that got here — 2–3 sentences, not a full career history. Pick the thread that's most relevant to *this* interview (backend depth, distributed-systems exposure, ownership) and follow only that thread; everything else gets cut, not because it isn't true, but because it dilutes the one the interviewer needs to hold onto.
3. **Future/Bridge (15–20 sec):** why this move, why this kind of role, and — when speaking to a specific company — one honest, specific sentence on why *this* company, not a generic "I admire your culture" line.

Total: 60–90 seconds. Practice with a timer; this consistently runs longer than it feels like it should on a first pass.

## A starter draft to adapt

This is scaffolding, not a script to memorize word-for-word — replace the bracketed pieces with the real specifics, and it should sound like natural speech, not a recitation, by the third or fourth rehearsal.

> "I'm a backend engineer with about three years of experience, mostly in Java and Spring Boot, with a fair amount of Kubernetes and Docker on the infrastructure side. Most recently at [Current Company], I've worked on [one concrete area — e.g., 'the services layer that handles X'], and along the way I picked up Angular on the frontend and Prometheus/Grafana for observability, so I've ended up being someone who can own a feature from the API down through how it's deployed and monitored.
>
> Over the past several months I've been deliberately going deeper into the distributed-systems side of backend engineering — I built a multi-module e-commerce platform from scratch specifically to get real, working experience with things I hadn't touched day-to-day: Kafka-based event choreography, circuit breakers, a Kubernetes/Helm deployment across three environments, and I ran actual chaos experiments against it rather than just reading about resilience patterns.
>
> What I'm looking for now is a role where that depth actually gets used at scale — [Company]'s work on [something specific and true — the platform's traffic profile, a specific engineering blog post, a specific technical challenge the company is known for] is exactly the kind of problem I want to be solving next."

## Two variants, not one

- **Recruiter-screen version (60–90 sec):** the draft above, roughly as-is — accessible to someone who isn't deeply technical, light on jargon, heavier on trajectory and motivation.
- **Hiring-manager / technical-round version (up to 2 min):** the same shape, but the "Past" section can go one level deeper technically — naming the actual architecture decisions on the platform project (Saga over 2PC, why the Gateway centralizes rate limiting) rather than just naming that the project exists. Save this longer version for when the room is technical enough to want it; using it in a recruiter screen usually reads as over-explaining.

## Common failure modes to check for

- **Starting too far back.** "I was born in..." or a full chronological CV read-aloud is the single most common way this goes wrong. Nothing before the professional arc needs to be in this answer.
- **No throughline.** A list of jobs and technologies with no connective "and that's why I..." logic between them makes the interviewer do the work of figuring out the narrative, which is exactly the job this pitch exists to do *for* them.
- **Sounding memorized.** A pitch that's rehearsed enough to be smooth but not so rigidly scripted that it sounds read — the target is "clearly practiced," not "clearly recited." Slight, natural variation between takes is a good sign, not a problem.
- **Ending flat.** No bridge to the role or the company means the interviewer has to supply the "so what" themselves. The Future section exists specifically so they don't have to.
- **Over-indexing on the Tier-3 college/company background as an apology.** This never needs to be framed defensively — the platform project and the depth behind it are the actual answer to any implicit "prove it" energy in the room; the pitch doesn't need to pre-empt a question that hasn't been asked.

## Practice Structure

1. Write a first full draft using the template above, filling in the real specifics.
2. Record it (Voice Memos or QuickTime, same tool already used for the Communication track's recorded explain-back sessions), timed.
3. Listen back once, alone, checking specifically for: total time, any "um"/filler-word density, and whether the throughline is actually audible or only visible on paper.
4. Deliver it live to the accountability partner, cold, no notes. Get feedback on the same three things, from someone who can't see the paper version.
5. Do one more pass incorporating that feedback, then set it aside — it should be ready enough to redeploy with minor company-specific tweaks (the closing "why this company" sentence) throughout the interview execution phase ahead, without needing to be rebuilt from scratch each time.
