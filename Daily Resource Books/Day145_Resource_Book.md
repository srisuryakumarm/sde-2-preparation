## Part 1 — Closing the Competency Map

### The Audit, Filled In

The plan's own framing for today is direct: go back over all eight stories together and check for coverage gaps — at least one strong story per competency, not a weak or borrowed one. Here's the primary mapping established across Days 141–144, as a starting point to confirm against your own actual stories rather than take on faith:

| Story | Prompt | Primary competency | Secondary fit (if emphasized differently) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tight deadline | Ownership | Navigating Ambiguity, if scope was also unclear |
| 2 | Disagreed with a manager | Conflict & Disagreement | Technical Leadership, if you drove the eventual direction |
| 3 | A time you failed | Failure & Learning | — |
| 4 | Went above and beyond | Ownership | Technical Leadership, if it involved influencing others' work |
| 5 | Critical feedback | Failure & Learning | — |
| 6 | Resolved a production outage | Navigating Ambiguity (per Day 143's flag) | Ownership, or Technical Leadership if you directed others during it |
| 7 | Pushback on a product requirement | Cross-Functional Pushback | — |
| 8 | Mentored someone | Technical Leadership | — |

**Coverage check:** all six competencies have at least one primary story. This isn't automatic good luck — it's the direct result of Day 143 catching that Story 6 was the plan's one unlabeled story and steering it deliberately toward the competency that would otherwise have had zero coverage.

**If, on reflection, any of your actual stories feels weak or borrowed for its assigned competency** — the plan's own words for exactly this situation — two options, worth understanding as genuinely different fixes rather than interchangeable:

1. **Re-angle, not rebuild.** Given the secondary-fit column above, a story that feels thin for its primary competency may already be a strong fit for a different one — Story 4's "above and beyond" framing, for instance, is legitimate Technical Leadership material if the actual content involved unblocking or influencing someone else's work, not just your own.
2. **Build fresh, only if re-angling genuinely doesn't work.** This is the last checkpoint before mock interviews and the real thing — better to notice a genuine gap now than mid-interview.

**🔑 Key Takeaway:** a story serving two competencies isn't a weakness in your prep — it's expected, and it's exactly why an 8-story bank can credibly cover six competencies with room to spare (2 extra stories beyond the minimum six) rather than needing a rigid one-to-one mapping.

## Part 2 — Google's "Googleyness and Emergent Leadership"

### What It Actually Is

Googleyness sits as one of four distinct attributes Google's hiring process evaluates — <cite index="18-1">general cognitive ability, leadership, Googleyness, and role-related knowledge</cite> — evaluated separately from raw technical skill, not folded into it. The plan's own framing is accurate: it probes comfort with ambiguity, intellectual humility (genuinely being able to say "I don't know"), a collaborative instinct over lone-wolf brilliance, and real curiosity.

Beyond those four, <cite index="16-1">interviewers assessing this axis also look for positivity, respect and inclusivity, a lifelong-learning orientation, humility, being a supportive team player, and conscientiousness</cite> — worth knowing this fuller picture even though the plan's four-trait summary is the sharpest, most actionable subset to actively rehearse against.

**⚠️ Common Mistake:** treating this as a soft, un-prepared-for round because it isn't "technical." <cite index="12-1">Candidates have been rejected from Google specifically on cultural fit despite passing every technical round</cite> — this axis carries real weight in the final decision, not token weight.

### Mapping Your Stories

**Story 2 (disagreed with a manager) → Googleyness.** This is a strong fit specifically for the intellectual-humility and collaborative-instinct traits: a disagreement handled well demonstrates you can hold a position *and* stay genuinely open to being wrong, which is the exact combination this axis is probing for.

**Story 8 (mentored someone) → Googleyness.** A strong fit for the collaborative-instinct trait specifically — mentoring is a direct, concrete demonstration of valuing team success over individual credit, which is precisely what "collaborative instinct over lone-wolf brilliance" means in practice.

**💡 Interview Insight:** when telling either story in a Googleyness-specific round, it's worth explicitly naming the moment of humility or collaboration rather than leaving the interviewer to infer it — "I want to be honest that my first instinct was to defend my original approach, and I had to actively push past that" makes the intellectual-humility signal legible instead of implicit.

## Part 3 — Databricks' Own Leadership Principles

### The Full Picture, Not Just the Two Phrases

The plan names two phrases specifically — "working from first principles" and "seeking truth" — and those are genuinely the two most load-bearing for your mapped stories. Worth knowing the complete picture, though, since a mock interviewer or real interviewer may reference any of them: Databricks names <cite index="7-1">six principles — customer obsession, raising the bar, truth-seeking, operating from first principles, a bias for action, and putting the company first</cite>, evaluated in behavioral rounds <cite index="4-1">much the way Amazon evaluates its own leadership principles, with interviewers mapping questions onto these values nearly one to one</cite>.

**⚠️ Common Mistake:** reciting the principle names directly in an answer ("this really shows my first-principles thinking"). <cite index="3-1">The stronger move is technical substance and reasoning that's naturally compatible with these values without reciting them by name</cite> — naming the framework explicitly can come across as reverse-engineering the answer to the rubric rather than genuinely embodying it.

### Mapping Your Stories

**Story 5 (critical feedback) → "Seeking truth."** The plan's own mapping, and a precise fit: <cite index="9-1">seeking truth means embracing data-driven insight and being willing to recalibrate assumptions based on changing conditions rather than defending an initial position</cite> — a story about genuinely internalizing critical feedback is close to a direct demonstration of this principle in miniature.

**Story 3 (a time you failed) → "First principles reassessment."** Also the plan's own mapping: <cite index="9-1">operating from first principles means breaking a problem down to its fundamentals to architect a solution with lasting impact</cite> — telling Story 3 with emphasis on *how* you diagnosed the failure down to its root cause, rather than just what you patched, is what makes this mapping land rather than feel forced.

**💡 Interview Insight:** if a Databricks interviewer specifically pushes on "how did you know the data supported that conclusion, not just your intuition?" — a strong Story 5 or Story 3 answer has a real, specific piece of evidence ready, not a general appeal to "I just thought about it carefully."

## Part 4 — Atlassian's Five Values

### Treat This Like a Technical Round

Per the plan directly: Open Company, No BS; Build With Heart and Balance; Don't [Mess With] the Customer; Play, as a Team; Be the Change You Seek. The plan's warning is worth repeating exactly as given, not softened: technically strong candidates have reportedly been downleveled or rejected in this specific round for answering arrogantly or without preparation — this gets the same rehearsal time as a technical round starting today, not improvised on the spot.

### Mapping Your Stories

**Story 7 (pushback on a product requirement, done respectfully) → "Play, as a Team."** The plan's own mapping — the emphasis on *respectfully* is the entire point of this fit: this value isn't about being right, it's about disagreeing in a way that keeps the team functioning afterward, which is exactly what Story 7's Result (the relationship holding up after the pushback resolved) is meant to demonstrate.

**Story 4 (going above and beyond) → "Be the Change You Seek."** The plan's own mapping — this value rewards proactively fixing something rather than waiting to be asked or escalating it as someone else's problem, which is precisely Story 4's Action.

**⚠️ Common Mistake:** given the plan's explicit warning about arrogance specifically, watch the framing on Story 4 in particular — "above and beyond" stories are the ones most prone to sounding self-congratulatory (Day 142 flagged this same risk). In an Atlassian-specific round, that risk is worth actively guarding against, not just being generally aware of.

### Coding Exercise — Today's Actual Deliverable

The plan's own exercise for today: write out all three mappings above in your own words (not copied from this book), then pick your single strongest story and rehearse it three times, reframed for each of the three frameworks in turn, 90 seconds each. The point of the exercise isn't the writing — it's proving the *same* story can be genuinely reframed on demand for whichever framework a given interview actually uses, which is a realistic scenario (you often won't know which company-specific framework is in play until you're already in the room).

## Day 145 — Interview Questions

**Q1. What are Google's four hiring attributes, and where does Googleyness sit among them?** General cognitive ability, leadership, Googleyness, and role-related knowledge — Googleyness is evaluated as a distinct, separate axis from technical skill, not folded into either the cognitive-ability or role-related-knowledge assessments.

**Q2. Name Databricks' six leadership principles.** Customer obsession, raising the bar, truth-seeking, operating from first principles, a bias for action, and putting the company first.

**Q3. Why is reciting a company's named values directly in your answer ("this shows my bias for action") usually a weak move?** It can read as reverse-engineering the answer to the rubric rather than genuinely embodying the value — the stronger move is technical substance and reasoning that's naturally compatible with the value without naming it outright.

**Q4. Recite Atlassian's five values in order.** Open Company, No BS; Build With Heart and Balance; Don't [Mess With] the Customer; Play, as a Team; Be the Change You Seek.

**Q5. Why does Story 7 map specifically to "Play, as a Team" rather than just to Cross-Functional Pushback generically?** Because the value isn't about being right in the disagreement — it's about disagreeing in a way that preserves the working relationship afterward, which is exactly what Story 7's Result is built to demonstrate.

**Q6. Why is it valuable that a single story (like Story 4) can be reframed for three different frameworks rather than needing three separate stories?** Because you often won't know in advance which company-specific framework a given interview is actually using — the ability to reframe on demand is a more realistic skill to have rehearsed than three narrowly-purpose-built stories.
